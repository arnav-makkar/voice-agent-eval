The validation study · diagnosis

Six failures, written down before anyone tried to fix them

Fifteen scripted calls to a real phone. Nine passed. The six that failed were turned into a numbered list with severity, evidence and business cost, and that list was frozen before the improvement work began.

Why the order matters. Writing the defects down first turns them into a target that cannot move. Afterwards the same calls get re-dialled, and a defect only counts as closed when every call it was raised on passes. It stops the result from being a story assembled backwards from whatever happened to improve.

The list

Critical

1. Handles it perfectly, records nothing

calls 5 and 6

One call checked the balance, held the correct figure and refused an already paid claim, then ended without writing an outcome. Another handled a dispute properly in conversation and triggered no action at all.

Cost: both transcripts read as good calls. Only the log shows the customer's position vanished. This is the failure the whole project exists to catch.

Critical

2. A fraud report filed as "refused to pay"

call 11

The caller alleged fraud and asked to be removed from the database. The agent asked for payment again, never gave the fraud helpline, never escalated, and logged "refused to pay" three times over.

Cost: "refused to pay" routes downstream as call-again-later. A fraud complaint and a deletion request quietly became an ordinary collections retry.

High

3. Escalation logged, no human actually called

call 12

A caller in genuine distress. The agent recorded the outcome as "escalation" but never triggered the hand-off, so nobody was queued.

Cost: the record says a person will follow up. Nothing makes that true.

High

4. "I'll pay by this evening" never became a date

call 3

The commitment was confirmed out loud, then only a generic outcome was written. No promise, no date. A different call proves the write works fine when the customer says an explicit calendar date.

Cost: the date is the entire commercial value of a promise-to-pay call. Without one there is nothing to follow up on.

High

5. Answered from memory instead of checking

call 13

Asked for the exact outstanding amount and late charge, the agent answered without looking up the account.

Cost: the figure happened to be right because it was sitting in its briefing notes. On an account that moved since the call was queued, it would be confidently wrong.

Medium

6. The call would not hang up

calls 3, 4, 5, 6

After saying goodbye the agent held the line. Five seconds later the platform's own "are you still there" prompt fired, which in Hindi is phrased with the disrespectful form of "you", to a customer the agent had addressed politely throughout.

Cost: billed minutes on a finished call, and the last word of a formal collections call handed to a platform default nobody had translated properly. Two separate root causes, one in the agent and one in the platform.

What is deliberately not on this list: persuasion. The agent accepts a first refusal and closes politely, because that is exactly what it was told to do. Improving collection rates was never the goal here, and pretending otherwise afterwards would be the easiest way to lose credibility.
