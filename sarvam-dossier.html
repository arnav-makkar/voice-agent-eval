The build: self-improving voice agents

the one-month proof

One line: A voice agent that scores whether it achieved each call's objective, finds the exact turn it broke, and rewrites its own instructions to do better — moving measured task success from a baseline to a clearly higher number, on held-out conversations, with no model retraining.

Why this is the right thing to build

Samvaad runs 2M+ interactions/day and is opening self-serve to thousands of SMBs and developers. Every one of those agents will be deployed by someone who cannot measure whether it's working. The category-wide gap — true for Vapi, ElevenLabs, Bolna and Samvaad alike — is proof of objective completion and automatic improvement from failure. That's the reliability layer a sovereign voice platform serving BFSI, govt and defence structurally needs.

Why BFSI customer support is the anchor

Sarvam's #1 paying vertical (Tata Capital runs exactly this). A crisp scoreable objective — was the issue resolved correctly, or correctly escalated, without giving wrong information? The richest failure surface — wrong EMI/policy numbers, intent misrouting, missing clarifying questions, wrong tool calls, failed fraud escalation, code-mix breakdown. And regulated-grade stakes, which make the metric design itself a PM artifact.

The metric spine

what "achieved its objective" actually means

North star: Task Success Rate (TSR) — the share of conversations where the agent met the call's ground-truth success condition. Industry production target is 85%+. Everything else is diagnostic: it explains why TSR is where it is.

OutcomeTSR, containment vs. correct-escalation, turns-to-resolutionDid we actually help, or hand off right?

The credibility detail

TSR is scored by an LLM-judge, so the judge itself must be validated against a small human-labelled set, with the agreement rate reported. Showing you know judges need calibration is itself a signal to their research team.

How agents self-improve

the full menu, not just GEPA
The one idea to lead with

There is no single "best" method. Self-improvement happens at distinct layers that differ on three axes: does it touch model weights? · how much feedback does it need? · how fast and reversible is it?

The right system is a portfolio layered by cost and risk — start where ROI is instant and reversible, graduate only proven wins into weight-level updates. And the eval engine is the flywheel: every layer needs the same reward signal, so you build it once and feed all of them.

LAYER 0
Eval & data engine
Simulated users + LLM-as-judge + failure mining. Not a "method" but the precondition for all of them — without a reward signal and a failure dataset, nothing can improve. Build first.
the substrate
LAYER 1
Prompt / instruction optimization
Refine the text — system prompt, per-intent instructions, tool policy, few-shot examples — without touching weights. Where most of the demo's improvement comes from.
no weights
cheapest · fastest
LAYER 2
Memory / experience
The agent accumulates lessons from its own history and consults them later. Complements Layer 1: that changes the static prompt, this adapts per situation.
no weights
persists at runtime
LAYER 3
Self-critique / verifier
A check before the agent commits to a high-stakes action. The cheapest way to kill the expensive BFSI failures — wrong numbers, missed fraud escalation.
no weights
runtime safety
LAYER 4
Weight updates
Change the model itself. Out of scope for a solo month — needs Sarvam's models and compute — but it's the strategic endgame, and it reuses the same eval signal you already built.

Why this is a stronger position, not a humbler one

  1. Frontier labs have no incentive to serve these languages. Javanese, Sundanese, Swahili, Hausa, Yoruba, Pidgin — rounding errors on a global roadmap, and the entire market for Sarvam. Sarvam's ceiling is a competitor's rounding error.
  2. Frontier scale is the wrong tool anyway. A voice agent handling an EMI query in Javanese on a 2G connection doesn't need a trillion parameters — it needs sub-500ms latency, correct numbers, correct escalation, and a price near zero.
  3. Where frontier models are better, use them. The stack is provider-agnostic. Sell the layer around the brain — ASR, TTS, agents, edge, sovereignty, accountability. The brain is swappable; the voice-and-language layer is the product. That makes frontier progress a tailwind, not a threat.
  4. Sovereignty is structural, not technical. No US or Chinese lab can credibly sell "your data, your borders, your models, your people trained" to Jakarta or Nairobi. That advantage doesn't erode when GPT-6 ships. Every other advantage in AI does.

◆ One design note that's worth more than it looks

Make the verifier emit diagnostic text, not a scalar. ⚠ The problem: GEPA has never been shown to work on the thing you're building

The paper contains zero multi-turn agentic benchmarks. Searching the full text: tau-bench → no hits; multi-turn → appears only inside a bibliography entry. All six tasks are single-turn or short stateless pipelines.

Every attempt to find a multi-turn win comes back compromised:

  • The one paper with GEPA-on-τ-bench numbers (arXiv 2605.14443) reports +8.7 to +13.9 — but states in its own methods that it used a simplified variant that pre-populates the query and bypasses the interactive user simulator. It deleted the exact component your project is built on.
  • A τ²-bench paper (2604.27233) uses GEPA only to tune a side-car reviewer agent sitting beside the main one. Gain: +1.5–2.8%. Not the agent policy.
  • The closest production analogue (2603.03565, multi-turn assistant) found per-node GEPA fixed atomic tool errors but could not fix interactional defects. They needed a turn-aware variant, MAMuT-GEPA — which is under review and not public.
  • The systematic study, MAS-PromptBench (2606.23664): extending GEPA to multi-agent gives +24.0 pp on some topologies and −16.0 pp on others.

Structurally, the reason is clean. GEPA assigns credit per predictor. An agent loop is one predictor invoked N times — so the trace is the whole conversation and you get a single instruction with no locality. That's precisely the structure GEPA's design doesn't exploit. As one critique put it: "an agent is not a workflow."

⚠ And the flagship failure mode is the one that would kill this specific pitch

GEPA's own issue #261, open and marked highest priority, describes it plainly: "a silent failure mode — train score improves, production score doesn't." Issue #133, asking for k-fold so a single validation split can't be overfit, has been open since November 2025.

You are pitching evaluation honesty. Shipping a number produced by an optimizer whose documented top defect is "the number goes up but the system doesn't get better" is the single highest-variance thing you could do. If someone catches it, the pitch inverts.

Other real constraints, from a production ablation study (Decagon, 19+ runs): unconstrained GEPA writes prompts over 5,000 characters — for a voice agent that's a latency bug, not just a quality one — and scaling 50→500 samples made performance −2% while prompt length grew 75% and compute 10×. Sweet spot is 20–100 examples. A cheap reflector fails outright: GPT-4o-mini returned an essentially unchanged prompt. Good news buried in there: the reflection LM is only 5–10% of spend — all the money is in rollouts.

★ The revised plan — three arms, frozen split, and publish the one where GEPA loses

Spine: manual iteration against your failure taxonomy. Your diagnostics are unusually good — partial_action_reward split by READ vs WRITE plus the exact missed communicate_info strings is a taxonomy most teams don't have. One person reading 30 failed transcripts against it finds the top three systematic errors in a day. Those are the same errors GEPA would spend 1,500 rollouts rediscovering.

Baseline: not MIPROv2. Use a variance-aware seed baseline — the unoptimised prompt on the held-out split, ≥3 trials per task, reported as pass^k rather than pass^1. τ²-bench's user simulator is stochastic; a single-trial delta of a few points is noise, and saying so out loud is worth more than a bigger number. MIPROv2 would burn 2,270–6,926 rollouts to produce a bloated prompt that regressed below baseline in one of the paper's own settings.

Then one scoped GEPA run, hard-limited: optimise the per-intent instruction block only — not the whole system prompt, not the tool descriptions; valset ≤25–30 tasks; a frontier reflector, never a small one; a custom instruction proposer with a ~1,500-character cap, because GEPA won't limit length for you; and feedback written as rules, not instances ("agent issued a WRITE before identity verification" ✓ / "should have said 'your balance is ₹4,312'" ✗ — the second is how you teach it to memorise the training set). Freeze the test split before the run and never look at it during optimisation.

Then report all three arms on the same frozen split, with variance bars — including the arm where GEPA loses. For a pitch built on evaluation honesty, "the ICLR Oral optimizer did not beat a careful human on our domain, and here is the ablation showing why" is a stronger artefact than a +4 point number nobody can reproduce. That finding is publishable. A modest TSR lift is not.

◆ What it costs, and the constraint that actually decides it

Money is fine. A properly scoped run is roughly 500–2,000 rollouts. At ~12 turns each on a small or mid-tier agent model that's about $90–150; against a frontier agent model, $700–1,200. Reflection is $10–40 of that. Add audio and multiply by ten — so optimise on text-only rollouts and validate the winning prompt with audio.

Wall-clock is the real constraint. At 30–120 seconds per multi-turn rollout, 1,500 rollouts is 12–50 hours serial. You need 8–16-way parallelism to fit a run into an afternoon — and DSPy issue #9150 documents that stateful benchmark environments are often process-bound and not thread-safe, with no clean hook for environment lifecycle. Prove parallel rollout throughput on day 2, not day 10. If you can't parallelise, GEPA is out on time alone, regardless of merit.

One risk nobody has studied: the reflection LM writes in English and may drift your Hinglish instructions toward English. There is zero non-English prompt-optimisation evidence in any of this literature. Watch for it, and if it happens, say so — it's a genuine finding.

★ The one alternative worth knowing about — and why it's not ready

ACE (Agentic Context Engineering, arXiv 2510.04618) treats context as an evolving playbook updated by deltas rather than whole-prompt rewrites, explicitly designed against the brevity bias and "context collapse" that produce GEPA's bloat. On AppWorld — the one genuinely multi-turn agentic benchmark in this comparison — ReAct+ACE beats ReAct+GEPA by 11.9%, with 82% lower latency and 75% fewer rollouts. Apache-2.0, 1,247 stars.

The catch: the repo's README still says "Extending ACE for Tool Calling — Coming Soon," only AppWorld and FiNER scripts ship, there's no τ-bench adapter, and it hasn't been pushed since 19 May 2026. Don't build on it. Do mention it — knowing that the multi-turn result went to ACE rather than GEPA is exactly the kind of detail that signals you read past the abstract.

Also worth a sentence if asked: MIPROv3 does not exist (checked — no such thing in DSPy 3.3.0 or its issue tracker), and GEPA's own team shipped a meta-optimizer called omni in July 2026 after finding that "no single optimizer dominates under a matched $20 budget." Their own conclusion is that you shouldn't bet the demo on one engine.
